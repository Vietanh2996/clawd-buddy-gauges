# Visual storytelling, humor and creativity in tiny pixel space

Research notes for the Clawd knowledge base. Clawd is a tiny pixel crab (blocky body, two claws, two eye dots, short legs) that acts out what a coding assistant is doing inside a terminal status band. Every source below was opened and read during this research. Anything I could not confirm from a page I opened is marked UNVERIFIED. Quotes are kept to short phrases; everything else is paraphrase.

---

## 0. The core thesis in six lines

1. At 8-32 px you do not have room to *draw* a story, only to *cue* one. The viewer's brain finishes the picture (McCloud's closure, Kare's "tell me once and I remember").
2. Constraints are not the enemy of character, they are its source: Mario's mustache, Gromit's mouthless face, the Coyote's Acme-only toolbox, Tamagotchi's few black pixels.
3. A gag is a pattern plus a break. Establish, reinforce, subvert. The break must still be *connected* to the pattern.
4. Timing is where small sprites get their comedy: the pause before the fall, the held look, the beat of recognition. Duration and placement matter more than extra frames.
5. One prop, one asymmetry, one eye change usually carries more story than three extra details.
6. The best ideas come from constraints on *behavior* (rules for who the character is and is not), then verbs, not from "what can I draw".

## 1. Constraints as creative fuel

### 1.1 Mario's mustache: the constraint becomes the identity
Source: "Mario's Mustache Was a Hardware Trick", Retro-Replay (author not shown), https://retro-replay.com/marios-mustache-was-a-hardware-trick/
- Miyamoto's Donkey Kong-era Mario had a 16x16 sprite on arcade hardware, too little room for a legible nose and mouth. A bold dark mustache separates the nose from the face and stands in for the mouth. A hat avoids having to animate hair; overalls make arm swings read against the body; gloves define hands.
- WHY it works: when you cannot draw a feature, a *higher-contrast proxy* (a single dark bar) reads better than a poorly-resolved real feature. Costume choices are chosen for what they let the eye separate, not for fashion.
- Tension: the article leans on Miyamoto's own retrospective jokes as evidence, not period design docs (the source's own weakness). It also shows the constraint outliving its necessity: once hardware improved the mustache stayed, because it had become identity.
- Taking: Clawd's identifying features should be the *workarounds*, the things that exist because pixels were scarce. Do not "fix" them later.

### 1.2 Gromit: losing the mouth made the character
Source: "'Wallace and Gromit,' 90% Alone", Animation Obsessive (Substack), https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/wallace-and-gromit-90-alone
- Gromit lost dialogue early in shooting of A Grand Day Out, and Nick Park found the eyes and brow could carry him. Wallace's mouth was initially small; Park widened it while animating the word "cheese" into a huge "coathanger" shape. The design was discovered during performance, not before it.
- The production was also tiny: a script around 20 pages in which one paragraph (building a rocket) ate roughly a year and a half; Park had no video assist and could not see a shot for about a week.
- WHY it works: removing a channel (speech, mouth) forces all information into the channels that remain, and those become distinctive. An eye-only character has no way to lie, so every glance is information.
- Tension: the same scarcity nearly ended the project; constraint is fuel only if the scope stays small enough to finish.

Source: "Feat of Clay: Nick Park on Stop-Motion...", Carlos Aguilar, MovieMaker, https://www.moviemaker.com/nick-park-early-man-interview/
- Park keeps a house look: close-set eyes, wide mouth. For cavemen he made mouths move up and down rather than just widening, a goofier look. Long scruffy hair gives a readable outline. Villain concept: a pompous middle manager rather than a menace (comedy by lowering the stakes).

### 1.3 Chuck Jones's Road Runner rules: constraints on behavior
Sources: Rich Barrett, "Chuck Jones' Rules for Writing Road Runner Cartoons", Mental Floss, https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/62035/chuck-jones-rules-writing-road-runner-cartoons ; Drew Hayes, "Learning From The Chuck Jones Road Runner Rules", https://www.drewhayesnovels.com/blog/roadrunner
- The rules (from Jones's Chuck Amuck; one source counts nine, another eleven): the Road Runner never harms the Coyote except by being himself; no dialogue except "Beep-Beep"; all action in the American Southwest; all gadgets from Acme; gravity is the Coyote's main enemy; the Coyote is more humiliated than hurt; the audience should sympathize with the Coyote; he never catches the bird.
- WHY it works: a fixed rule-set makes every new gag legible instantly (you already know the physics and the toolbox), so the surprise can live entirely in the *variation*. "More humiliated than harmed" keeps the comedy from turning into pain. Causality stays on the Coyote, the comic engine.
- Tension: the Mental Floss piece notes Michael Maltese said he had never heard of the rules, so they may be Jones's own later framework, not a studio-wide practice (UNVERIFIED as to how rigidly they were used in production).
- Taking: give Clawd a short list of rules (see section 9).

### 1.4 Tamagotchi: a few black pixels, huge emotional stakes
Sources: Helen Gonzalez Brown, "Preteen Dreams", Disegno Journal, https://disegnojournal.com/newsfeed/preteen-dreams-tamagotchi ; "The Hatching of an Icon", JAPAN HOUSE Los Angeles, https://www.japanhousela.com/articles/how-tamagotchi-changed-digital-design-icon-japanese-tiny-toys-30th-anniversary-bandai/
- The original: a monochrome screen and three buttons. Mood is carried by mouth open/close (with sound off, compared to a cat meowing behind glass), plus an icon vocabulary: steaming pile, heart, meter, a skull on death, an angel floating off.
- Gags: the character that keeps smacking its lips and later does a mocking dance; discipline yields black "frustration" clouds that look like revenge flatulence.
- Attention as story: care tasks, flashing icons and neglect-leads-to-death make the narrative continuous and demand attention. The JAPAN HOUSE article calls the result "minimalism of design with maximalism of emotional impact" and draws a lineage from kamon/kanji compact symbols and from Hello Kitty's mouthless simplicity. Concrete numbers: concept sketched in about 15 minutes, released November 1996, production scaled to roughly 3 million units a month.
- Taking: Clawd's states can be icon-first (a status glyph that flashes when something needs attention) and gag-second (a petty reaction when ignored). The character "does a bit" only when the state is calm.

### 1.5 Susan Kare: 32x32 and 1,024 pixels of meaning
Sources: Priceonomics, "The Woman Behind Apple's First Icons", https://priceonomics.com/the-woman-behind-apples-first-icons/ ; Wikipedia, "Susan Kare", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Kare ; Centre for Computing History, "Susan Kare", https://www.computinghistory.org.uk/det/1792/Susan-Kare/
- Icons on 32x32 monochrome grids; fonts at 9x7 per glyph, using only horizontal, vertical and 45-degree lines to avoid jaggies. Sketched on graph paper first; her roots are mosaic, needlepoint and "minimal pointillism".
- Her icon tests: meaning, memorability, clarity; an icon works if you can tell someone what it is once and they do not forget. She prefers visual metaphor (scissors for cut) to labels, and treats icons like traffic signs.
- Humor is built into the system: Happy Mac / Sad Mac, the bomb for crashes, Clarus the Dogcow (a distorted dog-or-cow that the ambiguity made memorable), a pirate flag with a rainbow-apple eye patch. A 32x32 portrait of Steve Jobs with a mischievous grin was reported as instantly recognizable, though veteran designers had thought personality at that size impossible.
- WHY it works: the humor comes from *failure states and edge cases* (a crash, a print dialog) where users are tense, so a joke there is the most valuable. A metaphor that is slightly wrong in an endearing way (Dogcow) is stickier than a clean one.
- Taking: the "error", "rate limited", and "cache out" states are the right place for Clawd's most memorable gags.

### 1.6 eBoy: modules, not pixels
Sources: "Of Pixels And Peecols: An Interview With eBoy", TOYSREVIL, https://toysrevil.blogspot.com/2007/09/of-pixels-and-peecols-interview-with.html ; "Interview with eBoy.com", comeonsuperstar.com, 2014, https://comeonsuperstar.com/2014/05/21/conversation-interview-with-eboy-com/
- eBoy (Steffen Sauerteig, Svend Smital, Kai Vermehr; founded 1997) say low resolution forces abstraction and simplification, which they see as an advantage; they care more about modularity than pixels per se. Peecol began as reusable 36x36 pixel bricks. Asset library: 8,484 items. A detailed cityscape takes about six to eight weeks with three people, or years in spare time.
- Their scenes tell stories by *density of recognizable pop-culture objects* (supermarkets, toys, robots, cars) rather than by character acting: the viewer reads the world.
- Taking: reuse a small kit of props (coffee mug, laptop, tiny flag, zzz) across states. A prop library gives a new story per recombination, and keeps the style coherent.

## 2. Reading a gag in one glance: silhouette, props, icon logic

### 2.1 Silhouette and reduction rules for 16x16-ish sprites
Source: Creative Mila, "The Rules of 16x16 Pixel Art", Neta Studio blog (Oct 2026), https://neta.art/blog/16x16-pixel-art-rules
- 16x16 = 256 cells; a 1 px border leaves 196; a 1 px closed outline on a roughly 12x14 figure costs about 50 cells, roughly 30 percent of the drawing area, so outlines must earn their place.
- Silhouette tests: shrink to 50 percent (if it collapses into a rectangle, the pose is too neutral); describe the shape in a phrase ("hooded figure leaning on a staff", not "humanoid"); give one asymmetric protrusion to separate characters; check props as shadow-only shapes on light and dark tiles.
- Face budget: two eyes and a mouth need about a 5x3 block inside a 6x6 head interior; a 5 px wide head can carry a face, a 3 px head becomes a dot. Keep text out of sprites (a 3x5 glyph at 1 px spacing fits four across 16 px).
- Symmetry: mirror, then break symmetry in exactly one place (a covered eye, a loaded shoulder) or the character reads as a chess piece.
- Motion: at 14 px height a natural stride is about 2 px of foot travel, which can strobe; exaggerate the stride, animate a 1 px torso bob, use 3-frame bounces; rates around 8 fps (heavy) to 12 fps (brisk).
- Caveat: this is a commercial blog; treat the specific numbers as rules of thumb (they are consistent with other sources).

Source: WigglyPaint Team, "Pixel Art Animation Guide: Make Tiny Characters Feel Alive" (Jan 2025), https://wigglypaint.com/blog/pixel-art-animation-guide/
- Check the silhouette in one flat color before detail. Two to four colors suffice for small characters. State the motion as one sentence ("the ghost is drifting") and cut details that do not serve it. Add one secondary detail after the main motion works; "five details" make it unreadable. Anchor feet and shadow to stop melting. Idle breathing: 2-3 states; bounce: 3 poses (squash, stretch, rest).

### 2.2 The face as the cheapest, strongest icon
Sources: Scott McCloud, Understanding Comics (1993), via EBSCO Research Starters summary by Gabriel Romaguera, https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/literature-and-writing/understanding-comics-invisible-art-scott-mccloud/ and Wikipedia "Understanding Comics", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Understanding_Comics
- "Amplification through simplification": stripping detail makes a figure easier to recognize and identify with; the mind projects a face onto almost any dot-dot-line pattern. "Masking": a simple character against a richer background invites identification. Caveat: I read secondary summaries, not the book itself.
- Taking: Clawd's eye dots are the identification engine. Eye position, size (1 px vs 2 px) and a blink beat give you most of the emotional range; keep the rest of the body quiet so the eyes win. A busy background would fight this; a flat band is an advantage.

### 2.3 Emoji: compress, then re-amplify
Source: Melinda Ozel, "Mindful Design: Everything You Did and Didn't Want to Know About Emoji Design", Face the FACS (2020, updated 2025), https://melindaozel.com/mindful-design-everything-you-did-and-didnt-want-to-know-about-emoji/
- A tiny face must stay readable, so sets use a restricted pool of interchangeable eyes, brows and mouths with matched line weight and palette. After simplifying, designers *amplify* the expression again so it still reads.
- One eye shape can flip a joke: the hand-over-mouth face with smiling eyes reads giggly; with open eyes it reads shocked. Small cues (a mouth-corner pinch, an eye bulge) change the emotion; "every pixel counts". Disgust is hard (no nose to wrinkle), so designers add a prop (vomit).
- Vendors disagree on the same emoji, and usage drifts from the original intent (pouting face used as sassy), so test whether the viewer's reading matches yours.
- Taking: define Clawd's expression kit as a small, fixed set (eye variants x claw variants) and test pairs against each other. When an emotion cannot be shown with dots, add a prop.

### 2.4 Pictogram grammar
Source: "otl aicher pictograms: System", piktogramm.de, https://www.piktogramm.de/en/system
- Aicher's Olympic pictograms rely on a defined grid and reduction to salient details; the page compares uniform rules to grammar, supporting over 750 pictograms. Quality is "articulated in its simplicity".
- WHY: a shared grammar (same angles, same stroke, same proportions) lets viewers learn the system once and then read new symbols for free. Limitation: the page is functional, not narrative, and says nothing explicit about silhouette (my inference).
- Taking: every Clawd pose should use the same body grid and a consistent claw/eye vocabulary, so a *new* pose is readable from the first sighting.

### 2.5 Staging
Source: Wikipedia, "Twelve basic principles of animation", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation (summarizing Thomas and Johnston, The Illusion of Life, 1981)
- Staging: present an idea so it is "completely and unmistakably clear"; remove irrelevant detail. Exaggeration helps but too many exaggerated elements overwhelm. Secondary action should support, never compete (facial expression is often unnoticed during fast motion). Timing is drawings per action; light things react faster than heavy ones. Slow in/out puts more frames at the ends.
- Taking: one idea per frame; if the claw wave is the story, freeze the eyes.

## 3. Silent comedy: Keaton, Chaplin, and the gag as visual argument

Sources: Sharan Shetty, "How Buster Keaton Perfected the Art of the Gag", Slate (Nov 2015), https://slate.com/culture/2015/11/tony-zhou-explains-buster-keaton-every-frame-a-painting-on-the-art-of-the-gag-video.html ; kottke.org post on the Every Frame a Painting video (Nov 2015), https://kottke.org/15/11/buster-keaton-and-the-art-of-the-gag ; Wikipedia, "Comic timing", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comic_timing
- Slate summarizes Tony Zhou's video essay: Keaton's aim was to tell stories through physical action, using camera placement, ingenuity, and a refusal to fake stunts; the gags are small works of visual craft, still influential (named: Wes Anderson, Jackie Chan). Kottke recommends starting with the short One Week.
- Comic timing page: pauses (Jack Benny's long silent stare after a setup line), Keaton cited for One Week, One A.M. and The Playhouse, and a debate on whether timing is script or performance. Greg Dean: timing is created in response to the audience, not possessed.
- LIMITS: these sources gave no frame counts and said little about specific Keaton gags. Specific claims often made about Keaton (the falling house facade in Steamboat Bill Jr., the locomotive in The General, his deadpan face) are from memory and UNVERIFIED here. I could not fetch a Chaplin source; Chaplin points are therefore omitted.
- Transferable principles (my synthesis, labeled as such): keep the camera/stage fixed and let the object do the surprise; the deadpan face is a contrast generator (calm face, chaotic world); the gag must be physically *possible*, which is why audiences accept it.
- Taking: Clawd as deadpan. Eyes stay as two flat dots while the world misbehaves (the status text blinks an error, a claw knocks a tiny cup). The calm face against escalating chaos is the cheapest high-yield joke in 2 px of eye.

## 4. Comedic timing and the shape of a 2-4 frame gag

### 4.1 Setup, expectation, payoff, and the pause
Source: Jesse Bray, "Art College Crash Course, Lesson 23: The Secret to Animation Timing", Mr. Bray Labs, https://anarchy.mrbray.com/animation-anarchy/2025/3/19/animation-anarchy-the-art-college-crash-courselesson-23-the-secret-to-animation-timing-or-why-every-good-joke-has-a-perfect-pause
- Timing is duration plus placement; comic timing lives in pause, speed and reaction; a gag can live or die on a few frames. Rule of three: setup (normal), expectation, payoff (break it). Anticipation (a crouch, a freeze) makes a reaction hit harder.
- The coyote cliff gag: he runs off the edge, hovers, looks down, looks at the viewer, then falls. The *pause before the fall* is the joke. Exercise: a ball held in the air for 6, 12 or 24 frames; which is funniest depends on the drop.
- Frame-rate translation (my arithmetic, not the source's): at 12 fps those holds are 0.5 s, 1 s and 2 s; at the 80-160 ms per frame used in Slynyrd's cycles (Section 8), a 3-frame beat is roughly 0.25-0.5 s. UNVERIFIED as best practice for a terminal band; test on the real band.
- Tension: a longer pause sharpens a gag but drains momentum if overdone; the source gives no hard frame limits.

### 4.2 Acting: the thought before the reaction
Source: Jesse Bray, "Lesson 22: How to Animate Emotion", https://anarchy.mrbray.com/animation-anarchy/2025/3/19/animation-anarchy-the-art-college-crash-course-lesson-22-how-to-animate-emotion-why-characters-need-to-act-not-just-move
- Emotion on three levels: physical (posture), facial (a tense brow, a glance away), psychological (show a thought forming before the reaction). Pause before a big move so the character seems to have decided. Asymmetry (one brow) adds life; flat symmetry feels dead. "I'm fine" with fidgeting is a contradiction gag. Exercise: a four-panel, wordless story (open gift, confusion, fake smile at socks).
- Taking: a three-beat acting skeleton for Clawd: NOTICE (eyes shift or widen, 1 frame), THINK (hold, no motion), REACT (claws). The hold is what reads as thinking.

### 4.3 Rule of three and misdirection
Sources: Ken Levine, "The Comedy 'Rule of 3's'", https://kenlevine.blogspot.com/2017/07/the-comedy-rule-of-3s.html ; Chris Head, "Lesson 8: Misdirection", https://www.chrishead.com/post/2018/02/02/lesson-8-misdirection ; ScreenWeaver editorial team, "The Rule of Three in Comedy: Setup, Reinforce, Subvert" (Feb 2026), https://www.screenweaver.ai/blog/rule-three-comedy-setup-reinforce-subvert
- Two items set a pattern, the third breaks it, but must still *connect* to the pattern (Levine); concrete specifics beat generic ones; overlong setup loses the audience. Head: set up two readings, show one; the laugh-word goes last; telegraphing kills surprise; visual misdirection is real (a plain look that sets up a contrary reveal). ScreenWeaver: two beats are too few to lock a pattern and four without a turn is repetitive; the third beat should get its own line; use either subvert OR escalate in one unit; running gags go see, recognize, then change context; callbacks reframe a gag later.
- These are written for stand-up and screenwriting; the application to sprites is by analogy.
- Mapping to frames: Frame 1 = pattern (claw taps), Frame 2 = same pattern, Frame 3 = break (claw taps a different thing / misses / the key falls off). Or run the pattern across loops: taps the key twice per loop, third loop it hits the edge of the screen.

### 4.4 Tension about "rules"
Rules of three and fixed hold counts are tools; the ScreenWeaver and Bray sources both say to vary them. If every gag follows setup-reinforce-subvert the formula itself becomes predictable.

## 5. Surprise, subversion, juxtaposition, exaggeration

- Aardman's Peter Lord (interviewed by Tasha Robinson, A.V. Club, 2012, https://www.avclub.com/aardman-animations-co-founder-peter-lord-reveals-the-be-1798231000): early puppets with glass eyes looked dead; swapping to bead eyes on pins (swivel and blink) fixed it; bright eyes read as innocence. Realistic mouth movement looked understated; *exaggerated* mouth shapes read better. Comedy: establish a real, grounded character first, then play with it; the cited favorite gag works through an innocent character and an exasperated timed reply. Background extras are atmosphere, not to be looked at. WHY: the eyes carry life, and exaggeration compensates for the loss of detail from the medium.
- Juxtaposition recipe (synthesis): contrast of *scale* (tiny crab, giant task), *tone* (calm face, frantic scene), *register* (formal status text, silly pose). Kare's Dogcow and Happy Mac are juxtapositions of friendly vs. technical.
- Exaggeration needs a ceiling: Wikipedia's principles page warns that too many exaggerated elements overload viewers; Slynyrd's Mega Man analysis (below) shows one exaggerated element (the fist swing) is enough.
- Subversion types usable in a band: (a) expectation of a loop broken once every N cycles (rare variant), (b) the prop does the wrong thing, (c) the character reacts to the viewer or the meter, (d) scale reversal.

## 6. Wordless UI mascots and anthropomorphizing small things

Source: Wikipedia, "Kevan Atteberry", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevan_Atteberry
- Clippit's designer made more than 15 concepts (including a rabbit and a stapler); hundreds of designs were tested and the paperclip tested best. It was famously disliked on launch (Time listed it among worst inventions) yet became a nostalgic icon.
- WHY it matters: Clippy's loud failure was *interruption*, not the drawing: a wordless mascot should not demand attention when the user is working. UNVERIFIED as a documented design conclusion; this is my reading from general commentary in search results, not a statement on the page.
- Compare Tamagotchi: its pet asks for attention but the user opted in.
- Taking: Clawd lives in a status band, an ambient channel. Idle gags should be quiet and brief, and the "attention" signal (flash) reserved for events that the user needs (permission prompt, finished task).
- Anthropomorphizing small things works because we assign motives to anything with eyes and a goal (McCloud's face projection; Gromit's eyes). Give the object a single believable desire (the crab wants the cursor to move) and the gag writes itself.

## 7. Pixel GIF artists and micro-stories

### 7.1 Octavi Navarro: a frozen moment that implies a life
Sources: "Timeout: Octavi Navarro", Dribbble Stories, 3 May 2016, https://dribbble.com/stories/2016/05/03/timeout-with-octavi-navarro ; "De completo desconocido a trabajar con una leyenda...", Xataka, https://www.xataka.com/videojuegos/completo-desconocido-a-trabajar-leyenda-videojuegos-solo-ano-historia-octavi-navarro-pixels-huh-ron-gilbert (Spanish; I read via the fetch tool's summary)
- Pixels Huh pieces are single scenes, each a frozen moment hinting at a larger life. His favorite, The Fisherman's Daughter, tells its story through mood: a bleak home, a girl preparing a birthday party for her father, hope in the effort though success is doubtful. He admires very simple shapes with strong color control as a way to evoke mood.
- Xataka: his games run about 20 minutes or less and are self-contained; he taught himself by trying to recreate magazine screenshots; he worked on Thimbleweed Park (Ron Gilbert, Gary Winnick).
- WHY it works: the *incomplete action* (a girl mid-preparation) invites the viewer to supply before and after, which is the same mechanism as McCloud's closure.
- The pixelshuh.tumblr.com pages and a Medium/Retronator article about Pixels Huh returned HTTP 403, so I could not read his own process notes. No pixel counts or timings from him; UNVERIFIED claims about his animation workflow are omitted.

### 7.2 Thimbleweed Park as a constraint example
Source: Wikipedia, "Thimbleweed Park" (the URL .../Octavi_Navarro returned this article), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thimbleweed_Park
- Brief: look like an undiscovered 1987-era adventure game. Comedy comes through cursed clown makeup, genre parodies, and a deliberately simplistic in-fiction "wireframe world". Mostly relevant as evidence that a deliberately limited look supports jokes about the limit itself.

### 7.3 Paul Robertson and MiniBoss / Saint11
Sources: Wikipedia, "Paul Robertson (animator)", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Robertson_(animator) (a thin credits list; the Giant Robot interview link 404'd and other interviews were not opened); Game Anim, "MiniBoss: Pixel Art Tutorials", https://www.gameanim.com/2017/06/10/miniboss-pixel-art-tutorials/ ; Saint11 article index, https://saint11.art/pixel_articles/
- I could only confirm that Robertson did pixel-style adaptations of existing properties (Simpsons couch gag, Adventure Time title, Rick and Morty short) and that MiniBoss (Pedro Medeiros, Amora Bettany) published tutorials on animation planning, run cycles, attacks and effects. The pages are GIF-based and gave no usable text figures. Treat anything about their method as UNVERIFIED here.

### 7.4 Frame economy and keyframe courage
Sources: Raymond Schlitter, "Pixelblog 8 - Intro to Animation", SLYNYRD, https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2018/8/19/pixelblog-8-intro-to-animation ; "Pixelblog 25 - Motion Cycles", https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2020/1/23/pixelblog-25-motion-cycles
- He cut an 8-frame run to 6 then 4 frames; he removed the recoil frame first (close to the contact pose) and the high point last (it carries the motion). Frame duration doubled from 80 ms to 160 ms to keep energy. A 2-frame walk doubling as idle is a classic cheap trick. Mega Man's 3-frame run (4 positions) is cited as a model of exaggeration (the fist swung up). "Trust in your keyframes"; more in-betweens can make motion sluggish; 6-frame cycles work in most cases.
- Personality from observation: a child marches proudly, an elderly person shuffles, a drunk sways. Study real behavior (or here, *a developer's* behavior) and borrow the silhouette of the attitude.
- Tension: more frames give smoothness, not energy; loops expose bad pixel clusters because nothing hides them.

## 8. Environmental storytelling in tiny scenes

Search did not turn up a good primary tutorial for environmental storytelling in tiny pixel scenes (UNVERIFIED gap). Relevant evidence from opened sources:
- eBoy (above): story by a dense inventory of recognizable objects.
- Navarro (above): a frozen moment with props that imply before and after (a preparation under way).
- Mario (above): costume-as-readability.
- Principle (synthesis): at 16-32 px, a scene's story is made of 1-3 *props with obvious function*, placed so their relationship shows an action (a cup beside a sleeping crab, a tiny laptop with a glowing dot). The prop must pass the shadow-only silhouette test (Neta). If the prop needs a label, it is wrong.

## 9. How to come up with ideas

Only partially supported by sources; the rest is my synthesis and flagged.
- Behavior rules first (Jones): write 5-8 rules for who Clawd is and is not (see translation).
- Verb-first ideation (synthesis; WigglyPaint's "one sentence per motion" backs the format): write verbs, then pick the ones that read in 1-3 frames. Verbs for a crab: sidle, snip, peek, burrow, scuttle, tap, flinch, preen, bubble, juggle, nap.
- "What would it do if bored?" (synthesis): boredom animations are low-stakes loops that show personality: counts something, pokes the edge, mimics the cursor, builds a tiny sandcastle. Bray's 4-panel wordless exercise is a good format for each.
- Found material (Chris Head): keep a notebook of ambiguous words, mismatches and accidents (a crab walks sideways, a "claw" is also a command grabber, "snip" resembles cut). Ambiguity is a source of misdirection.
- Mine the constraint itself (Thimbleweed's joke about wireframes; Navarro's one-scene-one-life): let the pixel grid be a character in the joke.
- Playtest in the real size and the real context; Neta says detail visible only at 4x zoom is wasted at 1x.

## 10. Translation to a tiny crab (Clawd)

Rules (Jones-style), proposed:
1. Clawd never speaks; the status text speaks. 2. Eyes are two dots and are the emotion channel; mouth does not exist (Gromit/Hello Kitty). 3. Claws are the verbs (tap, snip, wave, hold). 4. Clawd is humiliated, never hurt (error states are embarrassment, not damage). 5. A small prop set only: cup, tiny laptop, sparkle, zzz, flag, bubble. 6. Calm face, chaotic world: eyes are the last thing to change (Keaton deadpan). 7. One idea per frame (staging).

Concrete gags (each 2-4 frames, my proposals, untested):
- Thinking: eyes drift up-left, hold 2 beats, one claw rises to the "chin", a dot (the thought) appears. The hold is the acting.
- Typing: claws alternate; third beat, one claw hits the same key twice and the eyes blink as if surprised. (Rule of three with a break.)
- Done: pause, two small claw raises, then the *deadpan* beat: eyes return to neutral as a single confetti pixel lands on the head.
- Cache out: Clawd checks an empty cup (setup), tilts it (beat), one eye squints (payoff). A prop does the storytelling.
- Rate-limited/waiting: the "Coyote hover" gag: Clawd steps off the edge of the band, holds in the air for 2-3 beats, eyes look down, look at the viewer, then drops one pixel and shrugs.
- Sleeping: breathing 2-3 states, a zzz that drifts slowly; occasional twitch of one claw (a dream).
- Bored idle: counts pixels, sidesteps off to the edge, peeks back. Crab sideways walk is the built-in joke: forward is the surprise.
- Reaction to attention: when something important happens, a flashing icon (Tamagotchi logic) rather than a big animation.
- Error: Sad Mac energy. Eyes become "x" or flat lines (one change), a tiny bomb or tilted claw, never a long animation.

Checklist for any new Clawd animation:
- Silhouette reads at 1x as a one-phrase noun-verb ("crab holding cup")?
- Is there one asymmetry (one claw raised, one eye different)?
- Setup, beat, payoff identifiable? Where is the hold?
- Is the prop recognizable as a shadow?
- Can I cut a detail and keep the joke? (Slynyrd: cut the recoil first, keep the high point.)
- Does it loop without a visible seam, and is the loop's rare variant (every ~Nth cycle) used for the surprise?
