# Constraints as character: Gromit, Road Runner, Shovel Knight, Mega Man

> **Read when:** a limit feels like a cage and you want to see it as a design material. **Template:** constraint → choices → sacrificed → what reads → what to steal (not copy).


## Gromit
Lost dialogue early in making A Grand Day Out; Nick Park found the eyes and brow could carry him. The design was discovered in performance, not before it. A character with one channel has no way to lie, so every glance is information. Caveat: the same scarcity nearly ended the production, so a constraint only works as fuel when scope stays finishable.

## Road Runner rules (Chuck Jones)
No dialogue but "Beep-Beep"; all gadgets from Acme; gravity is the Coyote's enemy; he's more humiliated than hurt; he never catches the bird. A fixed rule-set makes every gag legible, so the surprise lives in the variation. Michael Maltese said he hadn't heard of the rules, so they may be Jones's later framework.

## Shovel Knight
Kept the NES palette, 16x16 tiles and sprite-count discipline; broke flicker, added a few "cheater" colours, widened to 16:9. The audience feels "NES" from silhouettes, palette restraint and chunky animation, not from literal compliance. **Pick which rules carry the look and drop the ones that hurt play.**

## Mega Man
Blue was chosen because the NES palette offered the most shades of it, which gave room for detail (Wikipedia). Crouching was cut because it made projectile heights hard to judge. Gameplay readability beat animation completeness.

## eBoy
Low resolution forces abstraction; they think in reusable modules (36x36 "peecol" bricks, a library of 8,484 items). A small kit recombined gives many stories and one coherent style.

## What to steal
- **Name your limit and make it the brand.** (Clawd: squares only, no mouth.)
- **Write a short rulebook** for your character's behaviour ([gag garden](../clawd/gag-garden.md)), and treat breaking it as an event.
- **Break the rules that hurt the experience, keep the ones that carry the look.**
- **Build a kit, then recombine.**

Remember: the rulebooks above are *the artists' own*, adopted by choice. Choosing yours is the creative act.
